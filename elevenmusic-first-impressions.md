# First Impressions: ElevenMusic

I spent some time exploring ElevenMusic as both a listener and a creator. These are less a formal product review and more the questions and observations I found myself coming away with.

## UX / First Experience

My first reaction was how clear the experience felt.

The onboarding immediately combined genres I had selected into a personalized welcome experience, which made the product feel tailored before I had really done anything. There was also a prominent creation contest that made me want to try generating something almost immediately.

For a product with a potentially intimidating technology underneath it, the path from arriving to actually making something felt surprisingly frictionless.

That said, once I got further into creation, the number of controls and options became much more apparent. With a music background, I found that exciting. I could also see it becoming overwhelming fairly quickly for someone without the vocabulary to know what they were changing or why.

## Creation

I've previously done transcription work for AI projects where a musical ear was needed to identify and describe elements of recordings used in model training.

For my first ElevenMusic experiment, I took one of those kinds of detailed musical transcriptions and used it as an input.

The result was honestly a little eerie.

The generated track captured a surprising amount of the musical information I had described, and it did it incredibly quickly. The transition from prompt to completed track to something I could potentially publish was also extremely simple.

It immediately made me think about the importance of prompting in music generation. The more precisely someone can describe arrangement, instrumentation, dynamics, tempo, mood, and structure, the more useful the tool seems to become.

## Remixing

I also tested the remix experience.

I started with a fairly downbeat electronic track and deliberately gave the model somewhat conflicting direction: I wanted something that would hype me up for a very long run, while still functioning specifically as the warm-up track.

It handled that tension better than I expected.

The driving rhythm remained, but the remix created a much larger sense of build and momentum. Some of the newly generated elements were also where the "AI-ness" became more noticeable to me — certain sounds crossed into an uncanny territory that felt less natural than the original material.

The larger idea behind remixing is probably what interested me most.

Allowing a listener to participate creatively in a track while creating additional value for the original creator feels meaningfully different from simply offering another AI generation tool. It gives a casual listener something to do with music rather than only something to consume.

## Licensing / Music Supervision

My background in licensing and music supervision made me think about another use case almost immediately.

A huge amount of music supervision comes down to some combination of:

- the perfect song being out of budget;
- the rights not being available;
- a client wanting several very specific musical qualities that don't naturally exist together in one track;
- or needing music that does something extremely precise structurally.

Generative music could potentially fill some of those gaps.

Instead of searching indefinitely for a track that is simultaneously the right genre, mood, tempo, instrumentation, structure, length, and price, a detailed creative brief can increasingly become the starting point for generating one.

That creates obvious opportunities, although it also raises important questions around rights, creator economics, authorship, and where generated music belongs alongside existing catalogs.

## Personalization

The personalized Mix feature was probably the part that left me with the most questions.

I like the idea of a system learning from what I listen to and what I generate, although I found myself wanting the product to tell me what it thinks my taste actually is.

Something closer to a musical horoscope or taste profile would make the underlying personalization visible rather than only letting me experience the output.

The ability to manually describe your own taste for generation is also interesting because it introduces two potentially different identities:

**What the platform believes I like**

versus

**What I believe I like.**

I'd be curious to see how often those align.

## Questions I Came Away With

A few questions I'm interested in exploring further:

- How accurate do listeners feel their generated taste profiles are?
- What does ElevenMusic learn from generation behavior that traditional streaming platforms cannot learn from listening behavior?
- How might that data eventually be surfaced back to listeners?
- Could there eventually be a "Wrapped"-style experience built around both listening and creation?
- How does remix behavior change the relationship between an artist and a listener, does it create a worthwhile feedback loop for the original artist?
- When does AI-assisted creation feel empowering versus overwhelming for someone without musical training?
- Where is the line between an AI-generated track sounding polished and sounding uncanny?
- Could aggregated generation and listening behavior become an entirely new kind of demand signal for the music business?

That last question is the one I keep coming back to.

If a platform can understand not only what people listen to, but the music they actively ask to have created, that may reveal something fundamentally different about demand.

Could generated music eventually function almost like rapid prototyping?

A concept could be generated, audiences could interact with it, and human writers, producers, artists, or A&R teams could potentially learn from those signals and develop an idea further.

I'm not sure where that leads yet, but it's one of the more interesting questions the product raised for me.
